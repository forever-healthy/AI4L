## Motivation

<!-- This section was written after the rest of the document was completed, so it reflects the full scope of the review. -->

Urolithin A is a small molecule produced in the colon when certain gut bacteria break down ellagitannins and ellagic acid — polyphenols concentrated in pomegranates, walnuts, and berries. It has drawn attention as one of a short list of natural compounds shown to activate mitophagy — the cellular process that recycles damaged mitochondria — a recognized feature of biological aging in muscle, immune cells, and the brain.  

The picture is complicated by an individual-biology wrinkle: only a minority of adults host the gut bacteria needed to generate meaningful amounts from food, which motivated the development of a synthetic oral form. Randomized controlled trials have since examined effects on muscle function and mitochondrial biomarkers, with outcomes that are promising but not uniform and almost exclusively short-term.  

This review examines what is currently established about Urolithin A — its mechanism, human trial evidence, known risks, sourcing considerations, interactions, and the protocol used by longevity-focused clinicians — and where the evidence remains preliminary, contested, or commercially entangled.

## Mechanism of Action

Urolithin A is not produced by the body directly. It is generated in the colon when specific gut bacteria (notably members of the *Gordonibacter* and *Ellagibacter* genera) metabolize ellagitannins and ellagic acid from foods such as pomegranates, walnuts, strawberries, and raspberries. Once produced, it is absorbed systemically — largely as conjugated glucuronide and sulfate metabolites — and distributes to tissues including skeletal muscle, liver, and kidney.  

The primary mechanism of interest is induction of **mitophagy** (the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria) via the PINK1 (PTEN-induced kinase 1, a sensor that tags damaged mitochondria) / Parkin (an E3 ubiquitin ligase that marks damaged mitochondria for disposal) pathway. In parallel, Urolithin A activates TFEB (transcription factor EB, a master regulator of lysosomal and autophagy gene expression) and upregulates transcription of mitochondrial genes, engaging mitochondrial biogenesis alongside the clearance step. Secondary effects consistently documented in preclinical and human data include:  

* Activation of Nrf2-ARE antioxidant signaling (Nrf2 is a transcription factor that switches on cellular antioxidant defenses)
* Dampening of NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa B, a master switch for inflammatory gene expression) and associated reductions in circulating hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, a marker of systemic inflammation) and pro-inflammatory acylcarnitines (fatty-acid-carrying molecules whose accumulation reflects inefficient mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation)
* Modulation of cGAS-STING signaling (cGAS is a cytosolic DNA sensor and STING is its downstream adaptor; together they trigger an innate-immune response when DNA is misplaced inside the cell) driven by cytosolic mitochondrial DNA, proposed to reduce age-associated sterile inflammation

A competing interpretation holds that the human clinical signal is more modest than the preclinical mechanism suggests: the 2022 JAMA Network Open trial (Liu et al.) reported no significant improvement in prespecified primary endpoints — 6-minute walk distance and peak ATP production in a hand muscle — despite changes in blood biomarkers and secondary endurance measures. The strongest molecular effects may therefore be on systemic inflammation and mitochondrial gene expression, with downstream functional effects that are real but smaller than rodent models predicted.  

**Pharmacological properties (for the Mitopure synthetic form):**

* **Half-life:** Plasma conjugated metabolites, approximately 17–22 hours (slow clearance dominated by the glucuronide)
* **Selectivity:** No selective receptor target; acts broadly through mitochondrial quality-control pathways
* **Tissue distribution:** Reaches liver, kidney, skeletal muscle, and heart in preclinical models; lower but measurable brain levels in rodents; human cerebrospinal fluid penetration is being characterized ([NCT07517913](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07517913))
* **Metabolism:** Phase II conjugation — glucuronidation via UGT1A enzymes (UDP-glucuronosyltransferases that attach glucuronic acid to facilitate excretion) and sulfation via SULT enzymes; not a notable CYP450 (cytochrome P450, a family of liver enzymes that metabolizes most drugs) substrate at physiologic concentrations based on current data

  
## Historical Context & Evolution

Urolithin A was first identified chemically more than 40 years ago as a microbial metabolite of ellagitannins, but for decades it was treated as a curiosity of pomegranate and berry chemistry rather than a therapeutic candidate. Interest sharpened in 2016, when [Ryu et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27400265/) published a paper in *Nature Medicine* showing that Urolithin A induced mitophagy, extended lifespan in *Caenorhabditis elegans*, and increased muscle function in rodents — the first demonstration that a naturally occurring gut metabolite could directly engage a core aging pathway.  

That paper catalyzed a translational program led by the EPFL spin-out Amazentis SA, which developed the synthetic form Mitopure to bypass the problem that most individuals lack the gut microbes needed to convert ellagitannins to Urolithin A at meaningful levels. The first-in-human safety and biomarker trial (Andreux et al., 2019, *Nature Metabolism*) established oral bioavailability and favorable changes in mitochondrial gene expression and acylcarnitines. Two subsequent randomized trials followed: an older-adult trial (Liu et al., *JAMA Network Open* 2022) and a middle-aged-adult trial (Singh et al., *Cell Reports Medicine* 2022). Later trials have explored athletes, immune function, cognition in obesity, muscle disuse, COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a progressive lung disease causing breathing difficulty), and prostate cancer.  

The field is still evolving. Early enthusiasm from the rodent lifespan extension has not been matched by equivalently large human functional effects, and the 2024 systematic review by Kuerec et al. explicitly notes the need for longer interventions and broader physiological endpoints before longevity claims can be confirmed. A substantial share of the clinical literature involves investigators financially affiliated with Amazentis SA, a relevant context for interpreting effect magnitudes rather than grounds for dismissal of the findings.  

  
## Expected Benefits

A dedicated search across clinical trial reports, systematic reviews, and recognized expert sources (FoundMyFitness, Huberman Lab, Life Extension, Examine, ConsumerLab) was performed before compiling the benefit profile below.  

### High 🟩 🟩 🟩

#### Muscle Strength (Lower-Body, Middle-Aged Adults)

In the 4-month MITOPURE trial in middle-aged adults (Singh et al., 2022, n ≈ 88, NCT03464500), Urolithin A at 500 mg or 1000 mg/day produced significant improvements in lower-body leg muscle strength relative to placebo. The effect is consistent with the proposed mitophagy mechanism and with rodent data on muscle function. The prespecified primary endpoint (peak power output) did not reach statistical significance, so the strength gain is a robust secondary-endpoint signal rather than a full confirmation of the primary hypothesis. The clearest effect was on knee extensor strength.  

**Magnitude:** ~10–12% increase in leg muscle strength at 500 mg/day and ~10% at 1000 mg/day versus placebo over 4 months

#### Reduction in Plasma Inflammatory and Mitochondrial-Dysfunction Biomarkers

Across the first-in-human trial (Andreux et al., 2019), the older-adult trial (Liu et al., 2022), and the middle-aged trial (Singh et al., 2022), Urolithin A significantly reduced plasma acylcarnitines (markers of inefficient fatty-acid oxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction) and hs-CRP. These shifts are dose-dependent and reproducible across populations. The Kuerec et al. 2024 systematic review also concluded that anti-inflammatory effects are among the most consistent findings across the human literature.  

**Magnitude:** Statistically significant reductions in multiple acylcarnitine species and hs-CRP at 500–1000 mg/day; absolute effect sizes vary by biomarker and trial

### Medium 🟩 🟩

#### Muscle Endurance in Older Adults

In the 4-month older-adult trial (Liu et al., 2022, JAMA Network Open, n = 66, 65–90 years, NCT03283462), 1000 mg/day Urolithin A significantly increased muscle endurance (contractions to fatigue) in both a hand muscle (first dorsal interosseus) and a leg muscle (tibialis anterior) at the 2-month timepoint versus placebo. The coprimary endpoints (6-minute walk distance and peak ATP production) did not achieve significance, so the endurance signal is secondary-endpoint evidence from a single small trial.  

**Magnitude:** Approximately 95 additional contractions in the first dorsal interosseus (a small hand muscle) and 41 additional contractions in the tibialis anterior (a shin muscle) versus near-zero change on placebo at 2 months

#### Aerobic Capacity (VO₂ Peak — Peak Oxygen Consumption — and 6-Minute Walk) ⚠️ Conflicted

The middle-aged trial (Singh et al., 2022) reported modest but clinically meaningful improvements in VO₂ peak (a measure of aerobic fitness) and 6-minute walk distance with Urolithin A, while the older-adult trial (Liu et al., 2022) reported a numerically larger 6-minute walk improvement on Urolithin A (~60.8 m versus ~42.5 m on placebo) that did not reach statistical significance. An 8-week trial in resistance-trained males (Zhao et al., 2024, J Int Soc Sports Nutr) found endurance and strength improvements, while a 2025 trial in highly trained distance runners (Whitfield et al., Sports Med) found limited benefit on running performance. The direction is plausibly positive, but magnitude depends heavily on baseline fitness.  

**Magnitude:** ~10–15% VO₂ peak improvement in middle-aged untrained adults; negligible incremental effect in highly trained runners

#### Immune Cell Rejuvenation in Aging

A 4-week randomized trial in older adults ([NCT05735886](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05735886); published 2024) reported that 1000 mg/day Urolithin A shifted peripheral immune-cell profiles toward a more youthful phenotype (higher naïve CD8+ T-cell frequencies, fewer exhausted cells, improved immune-cell metabolic state). This finding is consistent with the mitophagy-driven model of immunometabolic rejuvenation but rests on a single small trial and measures cell biology rather than clinical immune outcomes.  

**Magnitude:** Significant but single-trial shifts in immune-cell populations and metabolism; functional immune outcomes (e.g., vaccine response, infection rates) not yet measured

### Low 🟩

#### Cartilage Health and Osteoarthritis Symptoms

Preclinical work ([D'Amico et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35778837/), *Aging Cell*) shows that Urolithin A reduces cartilage degeneration and pain behavior in osteoarthritis models via chondrocyte mitophagy. Human data are limited to early clinical signal and manufacturer communications, not pivotal RCTs. Included here because mechanism and preclinical outcomes are strong, but the human evidence tier is not yet robust.  

**Magnitude:** Not quantified in available studies.

#### Cognitive and Neurological Support

Preclinical studies show Urolithin A attenuates amyloid-β and tau pathology and improves cognition in Alzheimer's disease mouse models ([Fang et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30742114/), *Nat Neurosci*), and protects against retinal neurodegeneration ([Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38890703/)). A human trial in obesity-related vascular cognitive impairment ([NCT05921266](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05921266)) has completed with preliminary results; a sleep and aging trial ([NCT06990256](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06990256)) is ongoing.  

**Magnitude:** Not quantified in available studies.

### Speculative 🟨

#### Cardiac and Metabolic Health

Animal models indicate Urolithin A preserves cardiac mitochondrial function, reduces pro-inflammatory lipids, and protects against doxorubicin cardiotoxicity ([Wang et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39725191/)). Human cardiovascular endpoints have not yet been measured in adequately powered trials; Kuerec et al. 2024 explicitly noted the absence of a human cardiovascular signal to date. The ongoing NIA (National Institute on Aging)-sponsored glucose metabolism trial ([NCT06274749](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06274749)) will help fill a key gap.  

#### Longevity and Healthspan Extension in Humans

Direct evidence for lifespan extension exists only in *C. elegans* (~45% lifespan extension in Ryu et al. 2016) and for endurance in aged rodents. There are no human lifespan or all-cause-mortality data. Kuerec et al. 2024 assessed Urolithin A as a geroprotector candidate and concluded that current human evidence supports biomarker-level effects; any lifespan claim at present is mechanistic extrapolation.  

  
## Benefit-Modifying Factors

* **Genetic polymorphisms:** No gene variant has been clinically validated as benefit-modifying for Urolithin A specifically. Variability in Phase II conjugation enzymes — particularly UGT1A variants (e.g., UGT1A1*28, UGT1A8 variants affecting glucuronidation capacity) and SULT1A1 variants (affecting sulfation) — is a plausible modifier of systemic exposure to the active aglycone, but has not been tested in Urolithin A trials. Variants in mitophagy-related genes (e.g., PARK2/Parkin variants) could in principle influence downstream response but remain untested in humans.

* **Metabotype:** Individuals fall into three urolithin metabotypes — Metabotype A (produces Urolithin A only), Metabotype B (produces Urolithin A plus urolithin B/isourolithin A), and Metabotype 0 (non-producers). Roughly 40% of adults produce Urolithin A efficiently from food, making dietary ellagitannin intake a poor substitute for direct supplementation at trial-level plasma concentrations. Metabotype is driven by gut microbiota composition, including *Gordonibacter* and *Akkermansia* abundance.

* **Baseline mitochondrial function and age:** Benefits appear more pronounced in adults with compromised baseline mitochondrial performance (middle-aged and older adults) than in highly trained athletes with already optimized mitochondrial capacity. Whitfield et al. 2025 found minimal incremental performance benefit in trained distance runners.

* **Baseline inflammatory state:** Anti-inflammatory effects (hs-CRP reduction) tend to be larger in participants with higher baseline hs-CRP and acylcarnitines.

* **Sex-based differences:** Most trials have been mixed-sex and not powered to detect sex differences. The Liu et al. 2022 trial population was 75.8% women but did not report sex-stratified functional outcomes. Preclinical data do not suggest large sex-specific differences, but this remains under-studied in humans.

* **Age:** Muscle endurance benefits have been reported in 65–90-year-olds (Liu et al.) and muscle strength benefits in 40–65-year-olds (Singh et al.). Data in adults under 40 outside of athletic cohorts are sparse. For adults at the older end of the target range (75+), biomarker and endurance signals are present, but functional translation to falls, gait speed, or independence has not been directly measured.

* **Baseline protein intake and resistance training:** Mechanistic and expert commentary frames Urolithin A as most useful as an adjunct to adequate protein intake and resistance exercise, not a replacement. Zhao et al. 2024 reported additive effects when combined with resistance training.

* **Pre-existing conditions:** Obesity, chronic inflammation, and age-related sarcopenia (progressive loss of muscle mass and strength with aging) are plausible conditions in which benefit magnitude may be larger, based on mechanism and the Singh and Liu trials. Data in frank muscle-wasting disease, neurodegenerative disease, and cancer cachexia (severe muscle and weight loss in chronic illness) are preliminary.

  
## Potential Risks & Side Effects

A dedicated search of clinical trial safety data, the Heilman et al. 2017 safety assessment, the FDA GRAS notification documentation, and manufacturer product information was performed before compiling the profile below.  

### High 🟥 🟥 🟥

*None documented in human trials to date.*

### Medium 🟥 🟥

*None documented in human trials to date.*

### Low 🟥

#### Mild Gastrointestinal Discomfort

Across multiple trials, mild and transient gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort) have been among the most commonly reported adverse events. Incidence has not differed meaningfully from placebo in most trials, and no dose-response signal has been established within the studied 250–1000 mg/day range.  

**Magnitude:** Comparable to placebo incidence; isolated mild cases reported across trials

#### Mild Musculoskeletal Soreness

In the Singh et al. 2022 trial, a modestly greater proportion of musculoskeletal adverse events was reported in the Urolithin A arms, attributed largely to the pre/post muscle biopsy procedure rather than the compound itself. Standalone drug-related myalgia has not been established.  

**Magnitude:** Not quantified in available studies.

#### Headache

Mild headaches have been reported in some trial participants at rates similar to placebo, without escalating severity or frequency over time.  

**Magnitude:** Not quantified in available studies.

### Speculative 🟨

#### Long-Term Safety Beyond 4 Months

All published human efficacy trials have run for ≤4 months. Long-term (12+ months) safety data in humans do not exist. Standard toxicology studies (subchronic, genotoxicity, reproductive) support safety at typical doses, and Mitopure has U.S. FDA GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) status, but extended human use at supplemental doses remains an open data gap.  

#### Theoretical Immunomodulation Risks

Given effects on innate immune signaling (cGAS-STING dampening) and on T-cell profiles ([NCT05735886](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05735886)), there is a theoretical concern that strong mitophagy induction might blunt desirable immune responses in specific contexts (e.g., active infection, vaccine response, immunotherapy). No human adverse signal has been reported, and the observed immune changes have been framed as restorative rather than suppressive, but this is mechanism-based caution rather than observed harm.  

#### Drug-Drug Interactions

Clinical data on interactions are limited. Urolithin A has not been characterized as a strong inducer or inhibitor of major CYP450 enzymes in humans, but formal drug-interaction studies are sparse. Theoretical caution with narrow-therapeutic-index drugs (e.g., warfarin, tacrolimus, cyclosporine) is reasonable pending more data.  

  
## Risk-Modifying Factors

* **Genetic polymorphisms:** No pharmacogenomic polymorphism has been clinically validated as modifying Urolithin A safety. Variability in Phase II conjugation enzymes — UGT1A (UDP-glucuronosyltransferases that attach glucuronic acid to facilitate excretion) and SULT1A1 (sulfotransferases) — is plausible as an exposure modifier but is unstudied in this context.

* **Baseline biomarkers:** Individuals with significant baseline hepatic or renal impairment have typically been excluded from published trials, so direct extrapolation of safety to significant hepatic or renal dysfunction is not supported by data.

* **Sex-based differences:** Safety profiles have appeared broadly similar in men and women in mixed-sex trials, but no dedicated sex-stratified safety analyses have been published.

* **Pre-existing conditions:** Pregnant and lactating individuals, children, and adolescents have not been studied and should be considered uncharacterized populations. Individuals with active cancer under treatment are being studied only in one registered oncology trial ([NCT06022822](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06022822), prostate cancer); generalized use during cancer therapy is not established as safe.

* **Age:** Trials span approximately 40–90 years old with similar safety profiles across the range. Adults outside this range (especially those over 90 or under 18) are uncharacterized.

  
## Key Interactions & Contraindications

* **Narrow-therapeutic-index prescription drugs:** Formal interaction studies are lacking. Representative drugs of theoretical concern include warfarin (an anticoagulant — a blood thinner), tacrolimus and cyclosporine (immunosuppressants — drugs that dampen the immune system after transplant), and some antiepileptics such as phenytoin (drugs that prevent or control seizures). **Severity:** Caution / monitor. **Consequence:** Theoretical changes in drug exposure. **Mitigation:** Separate timing by several hours and monitor standard drug levels or clinical endpoints (e.g., INR — international normalized ratio, the standard measure of warfarin anticoagulation) where applicable.

* **Over-the-counter medications:** No clinically significant OTC drug interactions have been reported. Ellagitannin-rich foods such as pomegranate juice are known to interact with some CYP3A4 (cytochrome P450 3A4, a major drug-metabolizing liver enzyme) substrates — statins (cholesterol-lowering drugs) and some calcium channel blockers (blood-pressure medications that relax arterial smooth muscle) — but isolated Urolithin A is not equivalent to pomegranate juice; the juice-level interactions are attributed to other polyphenols. **Severity:** None established. **Consequence:** No clinically significant effect expected from isolated Urolithin A.

* **Supplement interactions:** No documented adverse interactions with common longevity supplements (creatine, whey protein, omega-3, CoQ10, NAD+ precursors). Zhao et al. 2024 combined Urolithin A with resistance training and standard nutrition without adverse interaction. Theoretical additive effects with other mitophagy/autophagy inducers (e.g., spermidine) are plausible but unstudied. **Severity:** None established. **Consequence:** No documented clinical harm.

* **Additive-effect supplements:** Other mitophagy or mitochondrial-quality-control activators — spermidine, NAD+ precursors (NMN — nicotinamide mononucleotide; NR — nicotinamide riboside), and CoQ10 — may have overlapping cellular effects. No additive-harm signal is documented, but the cumulative magnitude and safety of stacking are unknown. **Severity:** Theoretical, low. **Consequence:** Unknown cumulative effect on mitochondrial quality-control pathways.

* **Other interventions:** Synergy with resistance training and adequate protein intake is supported by mechanism and preliminary evidence (Zhao et al. 2024). No incompatibility with specific dietary patterns has been identified. **Severity:** None (potentiating, not adverse). **Consequence:** Likely enhances functional benefit when paired with training and sufficient protein.

* **Populations who should avoid:** Pregnant or breastfeeding individuals (not studied). Children and adolescents under 18 (not studied). Individuals with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh Class B or C) or severe renal impairment (eGFR — estimated glomerular filtration rate — below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², CKD (chronic kidney disease) stage 4–5) — excluded from published trials. Individuals on active chemotherapy or immunotherapy (outside the prostate-cancer trial [NCT06022822](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06022822), where it is being studied under supervision).

  
## Risk Mitigation Strategies

* **Start at a lower dose:** Begin at 250–500 mg/day for 1–2 weeks before titrating to the studied 500–1000 mg/day range. This mitigates the possibility of mild gastrointestinal discomfort and allows tolerance assessment before committing to the higher dose.

* **Take with food:** Administer Urolithin A with a meal, ideally one containing some dietary fat. This mitigates mild gastrointestinal discomfort and may improve the plasma exposure of the glucuronide-conjugated metabolites that drive systemic activity.

* **Avoid in untested populations:** Do not use during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or in children and adolescents, because no safety data exist in these populations. This mitigates unknown developmental, reproductive, and pediatric risks.

* **Monitor during concurrent narrow-therapeutic-index drug use:** If used alongside warfarin, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, or certain antiepileptics, increase the frequency of the standard therapeutic monitoring already required for these drugs (e.g., INR every 1–2 weeks for the first 1–2 months on warfarin). This mitigates theoretical CYP-related or transporter-related exposure changes.

* **Choose a tested product:** Because ConsumerLab and other independent testers have reported that many marketed urolithin A supplements contain little of the labeled compound, choose products that publish credible third-party potency and purity certificates of analysis and, ideally, that use the same ingredient employed in clinical trials (Mitopure by Amazentis, which carries NSF certification and U.S. FDA GRAS status). This mitigates the risk of under-dosing and unknown contaminants.

* **Reassess at 3–6 months:** Evaluate subjective energy, strength, exercise tolerance, and relevant biomarkers (hs-CRP, optionally an acylcarnitine panel) to decide whether continuation is justified for the individual. This mitigates continued expense and exposure without measurable benefit.

  
## Therapeutic Protocol

A standard protocol — as used in the published trials and followed by several longevity-focused clinicians — is outlined below.  

* **Dose:** 500 mg/day or 1000 mg/day of synthetic Urolithin A. The 500 mg/day dose showed the clearest muscle-strength signal in the Singh et al. 2022 middle-aged-adult trial; 1000 mg/day was used in the Liu et al. 2022 older-adult trial and the [NCT05735886](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05735886) immune trial.

* **Form:** Direct oral supplementation with the synthetic compound. Mitopure is the clinical-grade form used in all pivotal trials. Dietary approaches via pomegranate, walnuts, and berries produce unreliable plasma levels because most individuals are non- or partial producers (Metabotype 0 or B).

* **Timing:** With a meal, typically in the morning. The ~17–22-hour plasma half-life of conjugated metabolites supports once-daily dosing. No strong circadian timing effect has been established in human trials.

* **Duration:** All published efficacy trials ran up to 4 months. Shorter durations (4 weeks) have yielded biomarker changes (Andreux et al. 2019) but not consistently functional ones.

* **Competing approaches:** Dr. Gabrielle Lyon, as discussed on the Huberman Lab podcast, frames Urolithin A as part of a muscle-centric longevity protocol alongside protein intake (~1 g per pound of target body weight) and creatine. Clinicians associated with Amazentis emphasize mitophagy as the central rationale for use across middle and older adulthood. No clinical-guideline-level consensus exists yet.

* **Half-life:** Plasma conjugated metabolites, approximately 17–22 hours, consistent with once-daily dosing.

* **Single vs. split dose:** Both schedules have been explored; once-daily single dosing has been used in all pivotal efficacy trials and is the standard practical schedule.

* **Genetic polymorphisms influencing protocol:** Metabotype (A versus B versus 0) affects endogenous production from food but does not meaningfully alter the pharmacology of direct supplementation. No dose-modifying polymorphism has been validated.

* **Sex-based differences:** No clinically established sex-specific dose adjustment.

* **Age considerations:** Both middle-aged and older adults have responded to the 500–1000 mg/day range. Starting at 500 mg/day is reasonable across the adult range; 1000 mg/day can be considered if tolerated and if the older-adult endurance context applies.

* **Baseline biomarkers:** Individuals with elevated baseline acylcarnitines or hs-CRP may show larger biomarker responses, but the dose itself does not need to be adjusted on the basis of baseline labs.

* **Pre-existing conditions:** Caution in hepatic or renal impairment (data gap). Contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation due to absence of safety data.

  
## Discontinuation & Cycling

* **Lifelong vs. short-term:** Currently unresolved. The proposed longevity rationale implies potential long-term use, but all efficacy data are from interventions of 4 months or less. No long-term outcome studies exist.

* **Withdrawal effects:** None reported in trials following discontinuation after 4 months.

* **Tapering protocol:** No known physiological dependence or withdrawal; no taper is required.

* **Cycling:** Cycling has not been formally studied. A rationale for cycling — for example, periodic 1-month pauses every 3–6 months — would be theoretical, driven by a wish to limit cumulative exposure and cost in the absence of long-term data. Continuous daily use mirrors the published trial design most closely.

  
## Sourcing and Quality

* **Third-party testing and ingredient verification:** The dominant sourcing concern. ConsumerLab and other independent testers have reported that a large share of urolithin A products on the market contain little to none of the labeled compound. Prefer products that publish third-party potency and purity certificates of analysis.

* **Clinically validated ingredient:** Mitopure (Amazentis SA) is the form used in essentially all pivotal randomized controlled trials. It holds U.S. FDA GRAS status and NSF certification. Products using licensed Mitopure as an ingredient (e.g., Timeline-branded softgels, Pure Encapsulations Renual) have documented identity of the active compound.

* **Dose per serving:** Confirm the product delivers 250–500 mg of Urolithin A per serving so that trial doses (500–1000 mg/day) are achievable without excessive pill counts. Some lower-cost products label 1000 mg per capsule but use unverified non-Mitopure material.

* **Reputable brands:** Timeline (Mitopure) is the primary research-validated brand. Pure Encapsulations (Renual) uses Mitopure under license. CodeAge and Neurogan offer alternative formulations whose identity and potency have been less well verified in independent testing.

* **Food alternative:** Pomegranate juice, walnuts, strawberries, and raspberries supply the ellagitannin precursors, but produce meaningful Urolithin A only in the ~40% of adults who are metabotype A or B. They are a poor substitute for direct supplementation if clinical-trial-level plasma concentrations are the goal.

  
## Practical Considerations

* **Time to effect:** Biomarker changes (acylcarnitines, mitochondrial gene expression) appear within 4 weeks. Functional changes (muscle endurance, strength) were measured at 2 and 4 months in the pivotal trials. Expect to evaluate at 3–4 months for functional outcomes.

* **Common pitfalls:** Relying on diet alone (most people do not produce sufficient Urolithin A from food); using products that fail independent potency testing; expecting performance gains in already well-trained athletes (where the incremental signal is small); and expecting primary-endpoint-level effects (peak power, ATP production) rather than the secondary-endpoint gains actually observed in the trials.

* **Regulatory status:** In the United States, Urolithin A is a dietary supplement ingredient rather than an FDA-approved drug. Mitopure holds U.S. FDA GRAS status as a food ingredient. No prescription indication exists.

* **Cost and accessibility:** Among the more expensive single-ingredient longevity supplements. Mitopure-based products are commonly priced at ~$60–125 per month at the 500–1000 mg/day dose, materially higher than most vitamin or mineral supplements. Availability is broad through direct-to-consumer channels but limited in mainstream retail.

  
## Interaction with Foundational Habits

* **Sleep:** No direct disruption of sleep has been reported in trials. The ongoing NCT06990256 trial is explicitly investigating Urolithin A (and fisetin) on sleep and aging biomarkers. Mechanistically, improved mitochondrial function and lower systemic inflammation could plausibly support sleep quality, but human data are limited. **Direction:** Likely neutral to mildly positive. **Practical note:** Morning dosing is typical in trials; there is no published evidence that evening dosing disrupts sleep, but no reason to prefer it.

* **Nutrition:** Synergy with adequate protein intake is implied by mechanism (supporting mitochondrial quality in muscle alongside anabolic substrate) and has been explored in the McMaster muscle-disuse study (NCT05814705) combining protein with Urolithin A. Ellagitannin-rich foods (pomegranate, walnuts, strawberries, raspberries) provide the precursors for endogenous production, redundant for efficient producers and insufficient for non-producers if clinically relevant plasma levels are the target. **Direction:** Potentiating with protein. **Practical note:** Pair with a protein-containing meal; no specific foods to avoid.

* **Exercise:** Benefits appear complementary to resistance training (Zhao et al. 2024 combined Urolithin A with training in resistance-trained men) and endurance training (Singh et al. 2022 measured aerobic endurance improvement), without the blunting of training adaptations that has been observed with high-dose antioxidant supplementation. Highly trained athletes show smaller incremental effects (Whitfield et al. 2025). **Direction:** Potentiating, more impactful in detrained or aging populations. **Practical note:** Does not require timing around workouts.

* **Stress management:** Preclinical data (Mallet et al. 2025) suggest Urolithin A rescues mitochondria-related transcriptomic signatures and reduces anxiety-like behavior in rodents, but no human stress or anxiety trials have been published. **Direction:** Likely neutral; possibly mildly supportive via systemic inflammation reduction. **Practical note:** Not a primary intervention for stress or mood.

  
## Monitoring Protocol & Defining Success

Baseline labs provide a reference point before starting Urolithin A, and periodic follow-up allows a data-driven reassessment of continuation at typical clinician-recommended intervals.

|-----------|--------------------------|------------------|---------------|
| hs-CRP | < 1.0 mg/L | Tracks systemic inflammation; expected to decline on Urolithin A | hs-CRP = high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. Conventional reference < 3.0 mg/L; functional practitioners target < 1.0 mg/L. Fasting not required. |
| Acylcarnitine panel | Within age-adjusted reference | Reflects mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation efficiency; multiple species decline on Urolithin A | Specialized panel (not routine); morning fasted draw preferred. |
| Fasting glucose | 70–90 mg/dL | Baseline metabolic context; the NIA-sponsored [NCT06274749](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06274749) trial is evaluating glucose effects | Morning fasted. |

| eGFR | > 90 mL/min/1.73 m² | General renal safety monitoring | eGFR = estimated glomerular filtration rate. No renal toxicity signal; baseline and follow-up are reasonable. |

Ongoing monitoring: reassess at 3 months (to verify tolerability and capture first biomarker shifts), 6 months, then every 12 months while on supplementation.  

Qualitative markers to track alongside the biomarker panel:  

* Subjective muscular energy and fatigue during typical resistance or endurance sessions
* Recovery time between training sessions
* Sleep quality and morning freshness
* Joint comfort (especially in those with osteoarthritis)
* Cognitive clarity and stamina through the workday

  
## Emerging Research

* **Bioenergetics and muscle function follow-ons:** The original older-adult trial [NCT03283462](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03283462) (Liu et al. 2022) is complete; follow-on work is exploring whether higher doses, longer durations, or combination with dietary protein close the functional-endpoint gap.

* **Glucose metabolism in aging adults:** The NIA-sponsored [NCT06274749](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06274749) (n = 180, adults aged 55 and older) will test whether 8 weeks of Urolithin A supplementation improves glucose regulation — a key missing piece for metabolic longevity.

* **Sleep and aging biomarkers:** [NCT06990256](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06990256) is testing Urolithin A (and fisetin) versus placebo in 80 adults with sleep disturbance and aging concerns — the first trial to examine sleep as a primary outcome.

* **Prostate cancer biology:** The NCI (National Cancer Institute)-sponsored [NCT06022822](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06022822) URO-PRO Phase 2 trial (n = 90) is evaluating tissue biomarker effects of Urolithin A in men undergoing radical prostatectomy — an important test of both benefit and neutrality in a hormone-sensitive cancer, informed by preclinical colorectal cancer data ([Francisco et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41646627/)).

* **Muscle disuse and atrophy:** [NCT05814705](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05814705) at McMaster University is examining whether Urolithin A plus protein blunts muscle loss during single-leg immobilization — a translational model for hospitalization and injury recovery.

* **COPD rehabilitation:** [NCT06324214](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06324214) is evaluating oral Urolithin A during pulmonary rehabilitation — relevant because mitochondrial dysfunction in skeletal muscle contributes to COPD-related deconditioning.

* **CNS penetration and mechanism:** [NCT07517913](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07517913) will measure cerebrospinal fluid kinetics of Urolithin A in healthy adults — a critical input for the plausibility of neurological applications explored mechanistically by [Fang et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30742114/).

* **Neurological and psychiatric exploration:** A rodent study ([Mallet et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40752777/)) showed Urolithin A abolished high anxiety behavior and rescued synaptic mitochondrial signatures — a plausible future direction for anxiety and mood research.

* **Cardioprotection from chemotherapy:** Preclinical work ([Wang et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39725191/)) on doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity raises the possibility of supportive use during anthracycline chemotherapy, pending human studies.

* **Pulmonary protection from radiation:** Preclinical work ([Zhang et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40253768/)) on radiation pneumonitis suggests another potential supportive role during oncological therapy.

* **Geroscience trial integration:** The PROMETHEUS trial ([NCT07451496](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07451496), National University of Singapore) integrates Urolithin A with NMN, creatine, and other geroprotectors in a combinatorial precision-aging protocol — expected to clarify whether stacking improves on single-agent effects.

* **Risk of null or negative outcome replication:** Several of the strongest functional claims (lower-body strength, VO₂ peak improvement) rest on secondary endpoints from single small trials; independent replications powered to the functional endpoints could weaken the case if effect sizes regress. Ongoing work in trained athletes (e.g., the [Whitfield et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40839339/) line of inquiry in *Sports Med*) has already shown limited incremental benefit in highly trained populations.

* **Sponsorship and publication-bias checks:** Because much of the existing clinical literature involves investigators affiliated with the commercial developer of the synthetic form, emerging non-industry-sponsored trials — including the NIA- and NCI-funded studies cited above — carry outsized weight in either corroborating or tempering the currently reported effect magnitudes. Non-confirmatory results from independent groups would materially weaken the case.

  
## Conclusion

Urolithin A is a gut-derived metabolite that activates mitophagy — the removal of damaged mitochondria — and does so in humans at doses of 500–1000 mg/day. Across randomized controlled trials, it has produced modest but repeatable improvements in lower-body muscle strength, muscle endurance, and biomarkers of mitochondrial efficiency and systemic inflammation. The short-term safety profile up to four months is strong, with adverse events broadly indistinguishable from placebo.  

Important uncertainties remain. The main prespecified primary endpoints of the pivotal human trials — peak power output, maximal cellular energy production, and 6-minute walk distance — have not consistently reached statistical significance, the largest functional effects have appeared in middle-aged untrained adults rather than highly trained athletes, and no human trial has run longer than four months. Much of the current clinical literature has been sponsored by or involves investigators affiliated with the commercial developer of the synthetic form, a relevant context for weighing effect magnitudes. Independent testing has also shown that a meaningful share of marketed products contains little of the labeled compound, a reported gap between label and content observed in the current marketplace.  

Overall, for a proactive, risk-aware audience focused on mitochondrial and muscle aging, the compound is mechanistically compelling and clinically supported at a biomarker and modest-functional level. Evidence for broader longevity effects remains preliminary and mechanism-driven rather than established by direct human outcome data.
